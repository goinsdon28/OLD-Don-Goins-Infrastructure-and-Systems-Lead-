Week 14 — From Technician to Instructor

Overview

Week 14 began with an expectation that I would continue developing the technical and security

concepts surrounding my triage-script project while providing some informal technician

mentoring.

Instead, the week developed into something substantially larger.

I was placed into an actual classroom-training environment and spent three days teaching

technicians how to approach ticket triage, diagnose network-related problems, use

troubleshooting tools appropriately, and transition from classroom instruction into independent

work on the data-center floor.

By the end of the week, I had also begun training three more leads.

This was no longer informal mentoring.

It became a combination of:

Instructor work

Curriculum development

Operational coaching

Skills assessment

Remediation

Lead development

Shift continuity

Central Teaching Principle

One statement became the through-line for the entire curriculum:

“You're not diagnosing a ticket. You're diagnosing a problem.”

The information presented in a ticket is only the starting point.

The recommendation may represent a downstream symptom rather than the actual

source of the failure.

The technician therefore has two responsibilities:

1. Prove what you believe the problem is.

2. Prove that conclusion against what the original diagnosis said.

The diagnosis is also not static.

The working cycle became:

Observe → Diagnose → Act → Validate → Reassess → Update Diagnosis → Continue

Teaching Tools Through Purpose

One of the strongest lessons from the week was that technicians should not be taught tools as

isolated procedures.

For every tool, I began teaching five questions:

What is it?

What does it do?

What is its job?

How do you know it successfully performed that job?

And most importantly:

When do you use it?

Knowing how to operate a tool has limited value if the technician does not understand the

condition that should trigger its use.

This became particularly important when teaching fiber diagnostics, optics, cleaning, loopback

testing, CLI commands, health checks, and AI-assisted troubleshooting.

Teaching Optics Through Concepts

Instead of asking technicians to memorize isolated optical procedures, I described optics

fundamentally as:

Light carrying data.

That simple conceptual model made several other troubleshooting concepts easier to understand.For example:

• Why a drained circuit changes what the technician should expect to see.

• Why light levels matter.

• Why loopback testing works.

• Why a clean optical path matters.

• Why good light on one side and poor light on another changes where

troubleshooting should begin.

The objective was to replace memorized procedure with enough underlying understanding that

the technician could reason through unfamiliar situations.

Assess Prior Knowledge by Connection, Not Recitation

One of the clearest lessons from the classroom was the difference in starting skill levels.

The group contained technicians with significantly different backgrounds.

Some demonstrated advanced computer or coding knowledge.

Others had reasonable technical knowledge but limited triage experience.

Others needed help with basic navigation and workflow.

Simply asking questions about terminology would not reliably identify these differences.

A better assessment was:

Can the technician connect one concept to the next?

Rather than asking someone to repeat what a tool does, I watched whether they could determine:

When should I use it?

What evidence tells me to use it?

What should happen afterward?

What does the result tell me to do next?

Different Learners Needed Different Approaches


That exposed actual operational understanding much faster than memorization questions.

Another important observation involved visual and hands-on learners.

Several technicians appeared to understand pieces of the classroom instruction but could not

assemble the complete picture until we physically entered the environment.

Once they could associate the terminology with:

• Ports

• Optics

• Fiber

• Racks

• LEDs

• Devices

• Cable paths

• Actual troubleshooting conditions

the classroom discussion became much clearer.

Some technicians then began explaining the process to one another using the same reasoning

model I had been teaching.

That became an indication that the concept was transferring rather than simply being memorized.

Gate Independence by Understanding — Not Time

I did not want technicians released onto the floor simply because they had spent a predetermined

amount of time in class.

Before allowing independent work, I began using a simple verbal readiness check:

“What's your ticket?”

“What's your first step?”

Their answers quickly revealed whether they understood the workflow.

If they could identify the problem and explain the first logical action, they were generally ready

to begin working with substantially less supervision.If they could not, additional instruction was more appropriate.

Adapt the Cohort to Different Skill Paths

The differences in technician experience eventually made a single classroom pace inefficient.

Rather than keeping technicians who had demonstrated readiness in the classroom indefinitely, I

began separating the group according to demonstrated skill progression.

Technicians who were ready for greater independence were released into peer pairs.

Technicians who were earlier in their skill-development path received additional time on the

fundamentals and more direct coaching.

The principle became:

Do not force every technician to progress at the same pace.

Give each technician the instruction needed to reach the same

operational standard.

The objective was not to identify stronger or weaker technicians.

It was to recognize that technicians entered the program at different points in their development

and needed different amounts or types of instruction to reach independent readiness.

Remediation Lesson

Two technicians required additional remediation.

Both ultimately recovered and independently closed tickets the same day.

Another useful lesson emerged from that process:

Sometimes a technician who needs additional development benefits from finishing with another

instructor.

Changing instructors does not necessarily mean the original teaching failed.Confidence sometimes requires another voice saying:

“You understand it. Go do it.”

This became another tool available for remediation rather than continuing to repeat the same

explanation indefinitely.

Triage Workflow Left With the Cohort

Before leaving the training group, I documented the operating sequence they could continue

using.

Initial Ticket Review

Establish Current Evidence

Understand the Current Diagnosis

What does the system currently believe is wrong?

Determine whether the evidence supports that diagnosis.

At this stage, the technician may have enough information to move to the physical environment.

Follow the Evidence > When the Ticket Stops Moving

Return to:

Diagnosis → Activity → Evidence

Ask:

“What is the system currently asking me to prove?”

When to use it, how to ask the right question, and how to validate the

answer.


Validation

This week also provided the strongest practical validation yet of the training concept I have been

developing: Assess → Normalize → Train → Validate

Assess the technician’s starting point.

Normalize the critical baseline gaps.

Train the individual where they actually need development.

Validate that they can independently perform the work.

The experience reinforced another principle behind the model:

“The goal is not to make every technician identical. The goal is to bring

every technician to a common operational standard as efficiently as

possible.”

The classroom demonstrated why this distinction matters.

Ten technicians did not represent ten identical starting points.

They represented ten different combinations of:

• Experience

• Technical knowledge

• Computer ability

• Troubleshooting experience

• Confidence

• Learning style

• Equipment readiness

A single fixed training path could not address all of those equally efficiently.

Leadership Development

The week expanded again when three leads began participating in the training after lunch

Wednesday.

This changed the scope of the work.I was no longer simply helping technicians learn how to close tickets.

I was beginning to transfer the reasoning and training methodology to people who would

themselves be responsible for helping other technicians.

That introduced another level of responsibility:

Training the people who will train and support others.

Career Evidence

During Week 14 I:

• Ran a full three-day technician curriculum.

• Trained 10 technicians.

• Began training 3 leads.

• Produced 5+ technicians who independently closed at least one ticket each.

• Remediated 2 technicians requiring additional development, both of whom

subsequently closed tickets the same day.

• Reduced training dependence to approximately 2–3 floor escalations during the

entire third day.

• Developed and reinforced a repeatable troubleshooting philosophy.

• Adjusted teaching according to technician skill level.

• Used hands-on validation rather than classroom memorization alone.

• Left a written triage workflow so the cohort could continue functioning during my

This is no longer best described as ad-hoc mentoring.

It represents:

Instructor + curriculum-author + technical coach + shift-continuity work.

Week 14 Reflection

I entered the week expecting technicians to shadow me.

Instead, I found myself standing in front of a classroom.

By the end of the week, technicians I had trained were closing their own tickets, technicians who

required additional development had progressed successfully, leads had begun participating in

the curriculum, and the cohort had a documented troubleshooting process they could continue

using without me.

One of the most significant changes in my thinking was recognizing that teaching technical work

is not primarily about transferring procedures.

It is about transferring reasoning.

A technician should eventually be able to look at a condition I have never shown them before

and still ask:

What do I know?

What evidence supports it?

What do I need to prove?

What tool answers that question?

What did the result change?

What do I do next? 

When they can do that without me standing beside them, the training worked.

That is how Week 14 ended.
